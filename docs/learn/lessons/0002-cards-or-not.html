Is it a card?

Both of your misses in lesson 1 were about cards. By the end of this one you’ll stop reaching for a card by default: you’ll ask one question about the content and pick the container that answers it.

What a card is for

Nielsen Norman Group defines a card as a container for a few short, related pieces of information that links to more detail (NN/g, “Cards”). It works through common region: things inside one boundary read as one group, even when they’re far apart.

That makes cards good at one job: browsing a pile of mixed items you pick from. NN/g’s example is Pinterest, where every pin has a different shape and you’re choosing one to open. Your rules say the same thing more strictly: cards are for objects people pick, move or act on, like photos to open or outfit choices (V4).

Why they fail as the default

NN/g names the costs: card layouts play down ranking, and they’re harder to scan than a list. A grid gives every item the same size, so nothing says “this one matters most” and there’s no single column for the eye to run down. Fill each card with an icon, a word and a blurb and you have Taste Skill’s strongest tell: 1% of strong sites against 28% of weak ones.

Before drawing a box, ask: what is the reader doing with these items? Reading, comparing, scanning, choosing, or browsing to pick? The answer names the container.

Your own projects took this path

Version 1 showed your projects as tiles in a mosaic: the clash with K9 you were asked to settle. Step 2 turned them into an index with a preview panel, and the v2 draft sets the names large in a list. Each step answered the question better: people scan projects by name, newest first, then open one. That’s a list with detail one action away (V5, V7), not a set of objects to pick from.

Practice

Pick the container for each piece of content. Your first answer counts.

1. Your four jobs, with dates, newest first.

  1. Grid of equal cards
  2. Tabs, one per job
  3. Timeline with dates column
  4. Carousel of job slides

A sequence in time wants a timeline: dates in one column, roles in the next (V2, PT2). Tabs and carousels hide all but one job, which breaks Scan (P5).

2. Six phones compared on price, battery, camera and weight.

  1. Table, one row each
  2. Cards with spec bullets
  3. Feature plus short list
  4. Big statement per phone

Comparing across three or more attributes is what tables are for (V6): each attribute gets a column you can run your eye down. Six cards make you hold every number in your head.

3. Thirty hike photos that people open full size.

  1. Table of photo names
  2. Editorial rows by year
  3. Tabs, one per photo
  4. Cards, each opens photo

Here cards are right: each photo is an object you pick and open (V4). It’s what the Photography tab does. Cards aren’t banned; default cards are.

4. A studio’s three services, each a paragraph with a real project photo.

  1. Three equal icon cards
  2. Picture and text rows
  3. Tabs for each service
  4. A carousel of services

A few image-led items to read: picture beside text, sides alternating (V2). Icon cards would throw away the real photos for a generic icon.

5. Eight hackathon projects. One won a national award.

  1. Eight equal project cards
  2. A table of projects
  3. Feature plus a list
  4. Tabs for each project

One standout among several: show it large with its picture and story, then the rest as a list (PT3’s default). Equal cards would bury the award at the same size as everything else.

6. A settings page with twelve on/off switches.

  1. List of labeled rows
  2. Grid of toggle cards
  3. Tabs, one per setting
  4. Feature plus a list

Alike items scanned by label: rows, label left, switch right (V5). Phone settings screens have looked like this for years.

  1. Eyebrow above heading

Three equal boxes, each an icon, a word and a blurb, now dressed in soft radius and resting shadows (two more tells). Same pattern as lesson 1’s question 3, in different clothes.

8. Why are cards weaker than a list when people look for one specific item?

  1. Cards load slower than plain lists
  2. Cards break the keyboard focus order
  3. Cards fail color contrast on mobile
  4. Cards hide ranking and scan worse

NN/g: card layouts play down ranking and are less scannable than lists. The other three aren’t properties of cards at all.

9. Why did your projects move from tiles to a list?

  1. Tiles look dated on modern sites now
  2. People scan projects by name and date
  3. Lists load much faster on slow phones
  4. Tiles cannot hold a GitHub link inside

Visitors scan projects by name and recency, then open one. The content’s shape chose the container (V2: “let the content’s shape pick the composition”).

Make it stick

From memory: the question to ask before drawing a card, and the container for each answer. Then open this.

“What is the reader doing with these items?” Scanning → list or rows. A sequence → timeline. Comparing → table. One standout → feature + list. Reading image-led items → picture + text rows. Picking objects → cards.

This week, whenever you see a row of cards (on a site, in an app, in something Claude builds), ask what the reader is doing and name the container you’d use instead, or say why cards are right. In three days, take lesson 1’s quiz again without the sheet open.
