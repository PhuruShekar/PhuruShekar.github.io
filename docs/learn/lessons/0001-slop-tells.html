Spotting slop tells

By the end you’ll be able to look at any page, name its slop tells, and say what goes in their place. It’s the skill you’ll use most: on your own work, on what AI tools hand you, and on the component libraries you browse.

The average of every page

Ask a model for a landing page and you get the average of the pages it has seen: a centered headline, a small label above it, a row of icon cards, a soft gradient, thin lines, a monospace caption. Taste Skill’s author sums it up as Nothing in it is wrong and nothing in it was decided. (Taste Skill). That’s what a tell is: not an error, a default nobody chose.

Taste Skill’s author had a panel score 247 real sites, then compared how often the strong and weak ones used each habit:

HabitSites scored 7.5+Sites under 6.5
Icon-topped feature cards1%28%
Tiny uppercase tracked labels16%57%
Gradients22%52%
Shadows24%48%
Pill badges14%34%

The twist: monospace labels, “01 / 02” numbering and hairline grids are more common on award-winning sites than weak ones. That’s exactly why generators copy them, and why they now read as costume. They’re banned because they’re borrowed, not because they’re ugly.

Removing a tell is half the job. In blind tests, jurors preferred a rich page with tells over an empty page without them. Fill the space with substance: bigger type, the full content, real pictures, a field of color.

You’ve already caught this yourself: on 2026-10-19 you called the first overview page “slop” for its tile grid, mono eyebrows and numbered sections, and those became rules T2 and K9. The whole list, with replacements, is on the slop tells sheet. Skim it once, then close it and try the questions from memory.

What goes in their place

7. You delete a row of three identical icon cards. What fills the space?

  1. A different composition with content
  2. Three cards with new icons
  3. A soft gradient background band
  4. Thin divider lines between items

A list, a table, a statement, a picture beside text: a composition chosen for the content. The other three swap one ornament for another.

8. An eyebrow reads “Selected work” above the heading “Projects”. The fix?

  1. Make the eyebrow monospace instead
  2. Fold it into the heading
  3. Turn the eyebrow into pill
  4. Center it above the heading

“Selected projects” says both in one heading. Or drop the qualifier: the list shows they’re selected.

9. Monospace labels are more common on award sites than on weak ones. Why ban them anyway?

  1. They fail contrast checks on light grounds
  2. Monospace fonts load far slower than others
  3. Generators imitate them, so they look borrowed
  4. Screen readers announce monospace text much slower

Generators copy what award sites do, so the habit now signals “template”. It’s taste, stated honestly, not a measured penalty.

10. In blind tests, which change alone made pages lose?

  1. Using two typefaces, not one
  2. Setting body text at 18px
  3. Removing ornament without adding substance
  4. Keeping square corners on pictures

An emptied page reads thin. Jurors preferred a rich page with tells over a bare page without them.

11. Your v2 draft puts each section’s name in a small serif label in a left column. Why isn’t that an eyebrow?

  1. It is the heading, not above one
  2. It uses a serif face, not sans
  3. It is muted gray instead of accent
  4. It sits on the field color band

An eyebrow is a small label above another heading. The rail label is the section’s only heading, in sentence case, beside its content. Uppercase tracking would turn it back into one.
